What the report shows

Craft and Structure is the biggest opportunity. At 200–360 it is in the lowest band, and with 13–15 questions it is the largest domain. These questions test vocabulary in context, the purpose and structure of a passage, and how two texts relate. Most of the score gain in this plan is expected here.
Information and Ideas needs precise reading. At 420–480, the errors are likely in evidence and inference questions, where the right answer must match every part of a claim and wrong answers are often too strong or off-topic.
Conventions and Expression of Ideas are his relative strengths (490–540). Grammar and punctuation follow a fixed set of rules, so Conventions can improve fastest. It comes early in the plan to build confidence and secure points.
Starting point and target. The domain bands suggest a Reading & Writing score in the mid-400s. The goal for these 12 sessions is about +100 points in Reading & Writing. The diagnostic test sets the exact starting point, and the two practice tests measure progress against it.

3. Strategies he will learn

  • Words in context: cover the choices, find the clue in the sentence, predict a word, then match it. Watch for reversal words such as “although,” “far from” and “anything but.”
  • Structure and purpose: say what the underlined sentence does (example, contrast, qualification, conclusion) before reading the choices.
  • Evidence questions: the right answer supports every part of the claim. A choice that fits two explanations equally supports neither.
  • Inferences: choose what must be true. Eliminate choices with absolute words such as “never” or “could not” unless the text proves them.
  • Data questions: check that the numbers match the exact comparison in the claim (a share, a change, or a difference).
  • Grammar: identify the rule being tested (sentence boundary, agreement, tense, modifier) before choosing.
  • Timing: about 71 seconds per question. Skip a hard question, mark it for review, and return.

4. How progress is measured

1. The test format

Structure2 modules × 27 questions. Each module has 32 minutes (64 minutes in total). 2 questions per module are unscored trial questions, and the student can't tell which.
AdaptiveModule 1 has a mix of easy, medium and hard questions. A strong Module 1 leads to a harder Module 2, which unlocks the top scores. Module 1 accuracy matters most.
PassagesEvery question has its own short passage (about 25–150 words) and four choices. Some passages are poems, stories, or tables and graphs.
OrderQuestions of the same type are grouped: Craft & Structure → Information & Ideas → Standard English Conventions → Expression of Ideas. Within each group they get harder.
ScoringReading & Writing is scored 200–800 (half of the total 400–1600). No penalty for wrong answers: never leave a question blank.
TimingAbout 71 seconds per question. Grammar questions take about 30–45 seconds, so the time saved goes to the harder reading questions.

3. The core method (for every reading question)

  1. Read the question first. It tells you what to look for in the passage.
  2. Read the passage actively. What is the main claim? Where does it turn (“however,” “but,” “yet”)?
  3. Predict the answer in your own words before reading the choices.
  4. Eliminate wrong choices for a reason you can point to in the text.
  5. Choose and move on. If two choices are left after about 90 seconds, pick one, mark the question for review and return later.

4. The five wrong-answer traps

Too extreme“always,” “never,” “proves,” “entirely,” when the passage says “suggests” or “some.”
True but irrelevantAccurate according to the passage, but doesn't answer the question asked.
Half rightThe first half matches, and the second half is wrong or not mentioned.
OppositeReverses the passage's meaning. Common after “although” or “not.”
Outside knowledgeSounds true in real life, but the passage doesn't say it.
Worked example: trap spotting (diagnostic Module 1, Q9)

The text: teachers in Lindqvist's schools reported more collaboration, but test scores were not significantly different.

✗ “Both collaboration and test scores increased” is half right.
✗ “Test scores improved even though teachers collaborated less” is the opposite.
✓ “Teachers reported more collaboration, but scores were not significantly different” matches both parts.

5. Reviewing the diagnostic

  • Open the report and group his wrong answers by domain and skill. Expect most of them in Craft & Structure, as the official report suggests.
  • For each wrong answer, ask him: “Why did you pick it? Why is the right answer right?” Then label the cause: K = didn't know the content (rule or word), R = misread the passage or the question, T = ran out of time or rushed.
  • Check the question timer: long times on correct answers show a speed problem. ⏰ marks show answers given after time ran out.
  • Choose 5–6 wrong answers to review together. Leave the rest for homework corrections.

The one idea behind everything: clauses

Independent clause (IC)

A subject and a verb that can stand alone as a sentence.

The canyon is deep.

Dependent clause (DC)

A subject and a verb that can't stand alone. It starts with a word like because, although, when, if, which, who, while, since.

Because the canyon is deep…

The method: when the choices differ only in punctuation, find where the punctuation goes (the split point). Then ask: is the part before it a complete sentence? Is the part after it a complete sentence? The answer decides the punctuation.

Rule 1: joining two complete sentences (IC + IC)

✓IC. ICThe team mapped the canyon. Its deepest point is 2 km down.
✓IC; ICThe team mapped the canyon; its deepest point is 2 km down.
✓IC, and ICThe team mapped the canyon, and its deepest point is 2 km down. (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so)
✓IC: IC  /  IC — ICOnly when the second sentence explains or expands on the first.
✗IC, ICThe team mapped the canyon, its deepest point is 2 km down. (comma splice)
✗IC ICThe team mapped the canyon its deepest point is 2 km down. (run-on)

Remember: a period and a semicolon do the same job. If two choices are “X.” and “X;” with the same words after, both can't be right, so the answer is something else.

Rule 2: “however,” “therefore,” “moreover,” “for example,” “thus”

These are not FANBOYS, so a comma alone can't join two sentences with them.

✓IC; however, ICThe drug worked in trials; however, it failed in larger studies.
✓IC. However, ICThe drug worked in trials. However, it failed in larger studies.
✗IC, however, ICThe drug worked in trials, however, it failed in larger studies.
✓Middle of one sentenceThe results, however, were not repeated. (fine: still one IC)

Rule 3: dependent clauses and introductory phrases

✓DC, ICAlthough the species look alike, their songs differ.
✓IC DCTheir songs differ because their brains differ. (usually no comma)
✗DC. ICAlthough the species look alike. Their songs differ. (fragment)
✗DC; ICA semicolon needs a complete sentence on both sides.

Rule 4: the colon

A colon must come after a complete sentence. What follows explains, lists or gives an example.

✓The expedition needed three things: rope, lanterns and maps.
✗The expedition needed: rope, lanterns and maps. (“needed” isn't a complete sentence)
✗It included items such as: rope and maps. (never after “such as,” “including” or “like”)

Rule 5: extra information (nonessential elements)

Extra information that can be removed without breaking the sentence is enclosed by two matching marks: two commas, two dashes or two parentheses. Never mix them.

✓The novel, a story told in letters, was praised.
✓The novel — a story told in letters — was praised.
✗The novel — a story told in letters, was praised. (mixed marks)
✗The novel, a story told in letters was praised. (second comma missing)

Removal test: cover the part between the marks. If the sentence still works, the marks are right. Essential vs extra: “researcher Ana Ruiz found…” has no commas, because the name identifies which researcher. “Ana Ruiz, a researcher, found…” has commas, because the description is extra. Use which with commas, and that with no commas.

Rule 6: where never to put punctuation

  • Between a subject and its verb: ✗ The decision by the officials, was praised.
  • Between a verb and its object: ✗ The team found, a new species.
  • Between a preposition and its object: ✗ a story told entirely through, letters.
  • Before “that” starting a clause: ✗ She argued, that the data were flawed.

Hard SAT questions often put a long phrase between the subject and the verb to tempt a comma. Find the real subject and verb first.

Rule 7: lists

✓Simple listrope, lanterns, and maps
✓Items already contain commasrope, which was rated for heavy loads; lanterns, which ran on oil; and maps (semicolons separate the items)

Worked examples

Example 1 (diagnostic Module 1, Q16)

The research team spent three seasons mapping the canyon ______ its deepest point, they found, lies nearly two kilometers below the rim.

A) canyon,   B) canyon, which   C) canyon   D) canyon;

Split point: “The research team spent three seasons mapping the canyon” is a complete sentence. “its deepest point … lies nearly two kilometers below the rim” is also a complete sentence. IC + IC needs a period or a semicolon. A is a comma splice, C is a run-on, and B makes “which its deepest point lies” ungrammatical. Answer: D.

Example 2 (diagnostic Module 1, Q19)

The novelist's latest book, a story told entirely through ______ has been praised for its inventive structure.

A) letters—   B) letters,   C) letters;   D) letters

“a story told entirely through letters” is extra information that opened with a comma, so it must close with a comma. Answer: B. A mixes the marks, C puts a semicolon after a part that isn't a complete sentence, and D leaves the pair unclosed.

  1. Honeybees communicate the location of food through a “waggle dance” ______ the angle of the dance shows the direction of the food relative to the sun.
    A) dance,   B) dance;   C) dance   D) dance, and,
  2. Although the painting had been attributed to Rembrandt for centuries ______ a recent analysis of its brushwork suggests it was made by one of his students.
    A) centuries;   B) centuries.   C) centuries,   D) centuries:
  3. The spacecraft carried three main instruments ______ a camera, a spectrometer and a magnetometer.
    A) instruments:   B) instruments, such as:   C) instruments;   D) instruments, which
  4. The architect Zaha Hadid ______ was known for buildings with flowing, curved forms.
    A) Hadid,   B) Hadid;   C) Hadid   D) Hadid—
  5. The early results were promising ______ the researchers decided to delay publication until the study was repeated.
    A) promising; nevertheless,   B) promising, nevertheless,   C) promising nevertheless   D) promising, nevertheless
  6. The coral reef — home to more than 400 species of fish ______ is threatened by rising ocean temperatures.
    A) fish,   B) fish;   C) fish —   D) fish
  7. The committee members who reviewed the proposal over several months ______ agreed that it should be funded.
    A) months,   B) months;   C) months:   D) months
  8. The festival featured musicians from Lagos, Nigeria ______ Lima, Peru; and Hanoi, Vietnam.
    A) Nigeria,   B) Nigeria;   C) Nigeria:   D) Nigeria
Show answers and explanations
  1. B. IC + IC: “Honeybees … dance” and “the angle … sun” are both complete sentences. A is a comma splice and C is a run-on.
  2. C. “Although …” is a dependent clause, so it takes a comma before the main sentence. A period or semicolon would leave a fragment.
  3. A. A complete sentence followed by a list takes a colon. Never use a colon after “such as.”
  4. C. “The architect Zaha Hadid” is essential identification, so there's no comma, and no punctuation goes between the subject and the verb (“was”).
  5. A. “Nevertheless” is not a FANBOYS word, so joining two complete sentences needs a semicolon before it and a comma after.
  6. C. The extra information opened with a dash, so it must close with a dash.
  7. D. “members … months” is one long subject. Put no punctuation between it and the verb “agreed.”
  8. B. The list items already contain commas (city, country), so semicolons separate the items.
